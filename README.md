## Study overview

This study examines acute thermal responses in four ahermatypic cnidarian species from the Galápagos Archipelago: the deeper-dwelling octocorals *Pacifigorgia darwinii* and *Muricea plantaginea*, the shallow-water cup coral *Tubastraea coccinea*, and the sea anemone *Bunodosoma grande*.

Dark respiration was measured across an acute temperature gradient, and thermal responses were characterized using respiration rates, temperature sensitivity coefficients (*Q*10), Arrhenius activation energies (*E*), and thermal response curve fitting.

Cool-water octocorals exhibited unexpectedly high respiration rates and steeper thermal sensitivity than shallow-water species, yet steep sensitivity generally occurred without an early respiratory decline across the experimental temperature range. These patterns suggest habitat-linked thermal specialization and highlight the importance of considering respiration rate, thermal sensitivity, and response shape as distinct dimensions of thermal physiology.

**ABSTRACT:** Understanding how temperature governs metabolism is increasingly urgent under warming, particularly for sessile ectotherms unable to track shifting thermal gradients. In marine systems, the relationship between thermal environment and metabolic performance remains poorly resolved, with limited direct comparisons among sympatric species occupying distinct thermal habitats. We quantified dark respiration across a 20–36°C gradient in four Galápagos ahermatypic cnidarians: two deeper-dwelling octocorals (*Pacifigorgia darwinii*, *Muricea plantaginea*) and the shallow-dwelling cup coral *Tubastraea coccinea* and sea anemone *Bunodosoma grande*, categorized based on depth distributions and associated thermal regimes. Cool-water octocorals consistently exhibited higher respiration rates than shallow-water species across most of the experimental temperature range, despite occupying cooler habitats, contrary to the ‘hotter-is-better’ hypothesis. Temperature sensitivity coefficients (*Q*10) were markedly higher in octocorals (2.23–2.56) than in shallow species (1.44–1.62), consistent with the climate variability hypothesis predicting steeper metabolic responses in thermally stable environments. Arrhenius activation energies, estimated for each independent replicate, corroborated this pattern, with the two shallow-water species converging on indistinguishable values despite their taxonomic distinctness. Across fitted response curves, most replicates showed no evidence of declining respiration within the experimental range; peaked responses were supported in a majority of *P. darwinii* replicates, with estimated optima ~33°C. Together, these results suggest habitat-linked thermal specialization in which cool-water octocorals exhibit unexpectedly high respiration rates alongside steep thermal sensitivity. The energetic demands associated with elevated metabolic activity may be partly supported by nutrient-rich upwelling, while steep thermal sensitivity could increase energetic costs under prolonged warming.
